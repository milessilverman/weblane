How to use these estimates

For a study that actually estimates a constant crime-price elasticity β, a change from crime rate C₀ to C₁ implies the following scenario calculation. An offender-arrival dummy, a homicide event and a criminal-damage density coefficient require their own original units.

Home-price change (%) = 100 × [exp(β × ln(C₁/C₀)) − 1]
Annual willingness to pay is a flow; converting it to capital value requires an explicit time horizon and discount/user-cost assumption.
  1. Keep outcomes distinct. A host parcel’s ADU premium, nearby-home spillover, assessed value, land price, rent, annual WTP and business sales are separate outcomes. Lower rents can improve affordability even when owner values fall.
  2. Preserve treatment and sample definitions. Store crime type, denominator, study version, estimator, sample unit, SE/CI, event stage, distance definition, baseline provision and persistence. A block map does not make a ZIP/county estimate block-level evidence.
  3. Use one coherent benefit calculation. A project’s measured price effect may already capitalize its crime, shade, noise and access benefits. Do not add those same channels again. Interactions can alter marginal effects; a universal additive list of premiums is unsupported.
  4. Separate resource cost from funding. Full capital and discounted upkeep/replacement costs belong in the resource-cost denominator. A grant changes the payer’s outlay; it does not erase labor, land or materials. Publicly owned land can still have an opportunity cost.
  5. Use actual events and historical geometry. Announcement, construction, first operation and final acceptance differ. Record date precision. Use notices of completion, work orders or post-opening records; a planned past date is not proof of completion.
  6. Keep unknowns visible. An incomplete lifecycle record or missing local causal coefficient remains NR. These tables supply a source foundation and candidates for local estimation, not a completed ROI ranking.
