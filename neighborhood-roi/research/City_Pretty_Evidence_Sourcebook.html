20. Model rules and Westside implementation

Recommended design for City Pretty, synthesized from the evidence above; these are implementation judgments, not a new empirical study.

  1. Separate outcomes. Show change in sale values, rents/housing affordability, quality-of-life outcomes and public fiscal cash flow separately. A rent decrease can improve resident welfare. A landlord's loss and a renter's gain should not be hidden inside one score.
  2. Use observed scales honestly. Parcel sales and geocoded crashes can support fine spatial analysis; ACS is generally block-group/tract data, PLACES is modeled area data, AQS is monitor data, and ECOSTRESS measures surface temperature. A block-level display does not turn all inputs into block-level observations.
  3. Store a coefficient's conditions. Record original outcome/unit, treatment/control, design, N and sampling unit, CI/SE, pre-period, anticipation/opening dates, distance definition, decay, duration, income/density/tenure, amenity baseline, costs included and publication/access status. Missing uncertainty means lower confidence, not a zero-width interval.
  4. Estimate a counterfactual. Use repeat-sales/hedonic controls and local time effects, compare plausible untreated locations, and check pre-trends, concurrent projects, construction nuisance and selection. Separate announcement from opening. Present-day inventories alone cannot reconstruct historical treatment.
  5. Price the whole lifecycle. Start with design, surveys, permits/environmental review, land/ROW/easements, utilities, construction labor/material/equipment, owner management/inspection, contingency and escalation. Do not add labor a second time if it is already in the winning bid.
Lifecycle resource cost = full capital cost + discounted annual operation/maintenance + discounted component replacements + decommissioning/monitoring − residual value.
Public outlay is a separate calculation after grants, private contributions, fees and other funding. Financing also requires debt-service cash-flow analysis.
  1. Avoid double counting. A bus-lane/trees/sidewalk project may already be represented by one bundled study. Health, safety, accessibility and noise improvements can also be reflected in property prices. Do not simply add every reported premium or add capitalization to a full monetization of the same underlying benefits.
  2. Model saturation and disamenities. Count existing access and capacity, not just number of amenities. The third similar park may add less; completing a missing path connection can add more. Distinguish immediate-frontage noise/traffic/light from walkable access benefits, and check whether maintenance or insecurity can reverse a park's benefit.
  3. Do not equate appreciation with finance. Under California assessment rules, most market appreciation does not immediately produce equal assessed-value growth. Only a share of tax revenue belongs to any city/district. Use turnover/new-construction assumptions and legally available funding instruments before claiming real estate can pay for an improvement.
  4. Keep jurisdiction explicit. Palms, Mar Vista, Beverlywood and Cheviot Hills lie within Los Angeles, while Culver City/Carlson Park use Culver City approvals, budgets and police records. Santa Monica, Beverly Hills and West Hollywood have separate rules/data. Confirm utility service territory independently. Do not apply LA TOC across municipal borders.
  5. Do not erase residents. Show effects on current renters, low-income households, people with disabilities, children, seniors, businesses and unhoused residents. Gentrification measures are not automatically individual displacement; encampment disappearance is not permanent housing. Track recurrence, affordability and access as outcomes.

Local first build: use LA County parcels/AINs; validate a named-neighborhood layer separately; obtain actual completion histories and itemized costs; compute walking/transit access on a street/entry-point network; and begin with interventions where local cost and treatment evidence are available.
